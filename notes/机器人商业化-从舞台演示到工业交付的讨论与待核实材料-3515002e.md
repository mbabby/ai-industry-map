## 一句话概括

材料借人形机器人估值和商业化争议提出一个问题：机器人能演示动作，距离客户愿意长期付费还有多远？它随后转向工业巡检与世界模型，主张从具体任务、稳定交付和持续订单评价机器人。

## 主要内容

### 1. 展示能力与生产能力之间仍有距离

材料批评把遥操作、预编程演示与自主执行混为一谈。值得保留的判断是：应交代演示条件、人工介入程度，以及换场景后还能否完成任务。遥操作本身也可用于采集数据和远程作业，不能一概视作造假。

### 2. 商业化要看需求是否持续

材料质疑数据采集中心、投资方采购、活动租赁等收入的可持续性。可转化为研究问题：谁采购、谁实际使用、采购目的是什么、会不会续单？政府支持或投资方采购本身，不足以证明订单虚假或收入不真实。

### 3. 技术难点不只在模型

材料列举泛化、真实交互数据、硬件可靠性和维护成本。研究时应分别看任务边界、环境变化、人工接管、故障与停机，而不是只看一次演示成功。

### 4. 工业任务提供了较明确的验证场景

巡检等场景有具体工作任务，便于比较部署前后的成本和效果。但工业现场并非没有变化，换厂、换设备、换路线后的适配与运维成本仍要核实。

### 5. “世界模型”需要拆成可验证的功能

材料描述了现场建模、仿真训练、真实部署与反馈的流程。这是材料的技术叙述，不能直接认定某厂商已完整实现。应核实各模块做了什么、解决了什么问题，以及是否有实测效果。

## 可迁移的判断框架

- **客户**：谁使用、谁付钱，为什么买，是否持续采购。
- **任务**：在哪个场景完成什么工作，成功标准如何定义。
- **人工**：遥操作、异常接管、维护分别占多少工作量。
- **经济性**：设备之外，还要计算部署、改造、维护、停机与人员成本。
- **复制性**：进入下一个工厂时，哪些可以复用，哪些必须重做。

China's leading humanoid robot company opened at 1,100 yuan, saw its market value cut in half on the first day, and by the time this video was made, it had dropped from 1,100 to 450, a decline of about 60%. This represents the capital

market's true re-valuation of humanoid robots. You often see investors in the comments section mocking it: "Even pig-farming companies spend 11 times more on R&D than they do; this is just a fancy humanoid toy company." Or

saying, "They organized 10,000 robot competitions just to lure me in." The reason for this shift in market consensus is simple: after the hype of robot performances at galas, competitions, and concerts, everyone has finally wised up. We all know now

that current humanoid robots just look human and rely on remote control and pre-programming; they lack strong world-cognition and the ability to interact autonomously. They are still a far cry from the vision of future

robots entering homes to serve as nannies or housekeepers. In essence, they have powerful limbs, but their brains are simply not capable enough. But humanoid robots do not represent the entire robotics industry; in other

areas, the "brains" of industrial robots are actually developing faster and more pragmatically. For example, the event model from Shengtong Energy, which we will mention later, serves as the brain for the top tier of quadruped

robots, specifically for industrial manufacturing, power, oil, and security applications. So, while everyone knows the humanoid brain is far below expectations—and lags behind specialized, non-generalized industrial

scenarios—the funding and hype remain . Behind this strange phenomenon, we must first look at the capital circulation of humanoid robots, starting with companies establishing data-collection centers with local governments. This data-collection

center is a site where real robots perform repetitive actions, recorded to serve as training data for the machines . In this process, government funds enter the center, the robot company sells units to the center as revenue,

and the money flows to the company. The center then generates valuable data, which the robot company buys back, returning the funds to the center. Although the money just goes back and forth, the robot company obtains tangible orders and cash flow to

support its IPO expectations; this is the first type of capital circulation. The other type of capital circulation involves the investors who are also the clients. In fact, investors in major humanoid robot companies have also

appeared on the procurement lists. Their reasons for purchasing these robots vary, but many lack sustainability—it’s often a one-off purchase just to provide a short-term revenue boost for their invested companies, helping them on the path to

an IPO so the investors can quickly cash out at a high valuation after the listing. Beyond this cycle of capital, looking at the revenue sources for humanoid robots, income from rental businesses is also shrinking. During

the 2025 Spring Festival, humanoid robots went viral on the Spring Festival Gala, sparking a temporary craze across society. Everyone began renting these robots for performances, such as at tourist attractions, concerts, and corporate annual meetings

, which is when daily rental prices peaked. However, demand has since significantly plummeted, causing rental prices to drop as well. The situation has worsened further this year, confirming the unsustainability of this

particular capital-driven strategy. Regarding recent information, due to the massive negative impact caused by the stock market crash of a leading humanoid robot firm, regulators have issued "window guidance" to several

investment banks and institutions. According to Bloomberg on September 29, three sources familiar with the regulations revealed that the guidance focuses on company performance: first, you must have sustainable revenue and

commercial orders; second, losses must be narrowing, with projections covering the next three years; and third, you must possess core technologies, such as the robot's "brain" or joints, rather than just being an assembly shop

relying on outsourced parts. The market believes companies may only need to meet two of these three criteria, but even by this standard, only a small number of firms will be able to go public. In fact, according to public

information, at least 20 humanoid robot companies have filed to list on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange—meaning a crowd of companies claiming to be " humanoid concept" firms want to go public in Hong Kong. And these 20-plus

are just a rough figure; the actual number is likely higher. But listing in Hong Kong also requires CSRC registration, so they won't be able to just cut corners. I’ve also learned from people around me—an old colleague was reminded—that

regulators are very concerned about the authenticity of revenue in the robotics sector. It cannot rely on government subsidies, transactional gains, or a " data center" model for window dressing. Second is profit stability: if you made

some money these past two months, what about the future? Third is large-scale shipments and real-world application; shipment volume must be high, and those robots must actually be in use. That same month, Reuters released even more

negative news: multiple insiders stated that the IPOs of some humanoid robot companies might be blocked, and for those currently preparing to list, their primary market valuations have correspondingly fallen by 30%to 50%. This really highlights the

farsightedness of our regulators. In fact, as early as August, even before the stock market crash of the leading companies, the state had already seen the hidden risks. According to official information, a spokesperson for the

National Development and Reform Commission stated at a press conference that the industry must insist on adapting to local conditions to ensure healthy and orderly development, preventing blind trends and rush-to-invest behaviors. The next step

is to have robots iterate their technology in real-world scenarios and form application closed-loops around actual needs. It could be said that they anticipated the market's anticipation. With both the market and regulators seeing a sharp decline in

confidence regarding humanoid robots, the crash of leading robotics firms is no surprise; the core issue remains the industry bottleneck—the first hurdle is "generalization," meaning whether a robot can still function in a different

environment. For humanoid robots, as long as data is collected in a fixed scenario, many models can achieve a 100 %success rate, but the physical world is incredibly rich, diverse, and complex, and we cannot describe such a

complex world with a single set of simple rules. Once the scene, lighting, or objects change, data must be re-collected; if the items or environments the robot interacts with shift even slightly, the success rate

drops significantly. Dancing on a stage is indeed a fixed scenario with a high success rate, but is the purpose of building robots really just to dance? The second hurdle is data; there is nothing for their "brains" to learn. As

mentioned, the current intelligence of humanoid robots is in its infancy, so how do we train their brains? Large Language Models learn from text across the entire internet, which is why many of the AI tools we use today, like GPT,

iterate so quickly. But robots need to learn how much force to apply with their hands, how to land their feet, how to climb over obstacles in front of them, whether an object is hard or soft , whether they should gently cradle it

or grip it firmly, whether a sound signifies an object approaching or moving away, and what new forms objects might take at higher temperatures. This kind of data isn't on the internet; it can only be collected one by one using

real machines, which refers back to the data collection centers mentioned at the start. The total amount of high-quality, real-world interaction data in the entire industry is only about 500,000 hours, but at least 10

million hours are needed for usability; more aggressive estimates suggest that for a general-purpose robot to reach a 70%to 80%success rate on common tasks, 100 million hours are required, making the cost extremely high. Estimates from

Nomura indicate that 200,000 hours of data would cost over 200 million yuan, while reaching a million hours would be in the range of 1 billion yuan. The third hurdle: hardware, precision, reliability, and the cost of the supply

chain. The technical approach for robotic hands remains unified, with everyone still in a trial-and-error phase, costing between 40,000 to 50,000 yuan per unit. Harmonic drives, the precision transmission components

inside the joints, require a precision of plus or minus six microns; they are both expensive and difficult to manufacture, let alone meeting the industrial requirement of a five-year lifespan. These seemingly minor issues

are actually massive obstacles, each backed by huge R&D costs. Technical progress has plateaued, but for the sake of market buzz, everyone has started playing word games, such as blurring the lines between autonomous

intelligence and human operation—a tactic used repeatedly where companies seemingly admit to remote control while coincidentally leading you to believe it’s AI autonomy, at least as it appears on video. Take the case of

Tesla’s Optimus robot: on January 15, 2024, Musk posted a video of Optimus folding a shirt, captioned "Folding a shirt." Both the text and the footage suggested the humanoid robot was doing it autonomously, but at the 12-second

mark, a hand wearing a control glove is visible in the bottom right corner. This sparked a huge controversy, and half an hour later, he added a comment that neither confirmed nor denied the remote control, cleverly shifting the

topic by saying Optimus couldn't do it autonomously yet and needed a fixed table and a box to hold the shirt. The matter was then left unresolved. The most significant embarrassment occurred in late 2025, when Optimus was standing

behind a table filled with water bottles to hand them to the audience. However, a video from an attendee showed that when the robot reached for a bottle, it dropped and lost balance. Before falling, it lifted both hands,

wiped down the sides of its face, and then fell backward—a gesture identical to a human removing a headset . Many netizens speculated that the operator off-screen encountered an emergency, didn't have time to turn off

the system, and removed their own headset, which the robot then mimicked. Since then, the market has begun to widely question whether these humanoid robots are AI-driven or just remote-controlled toys. Even during the

Q2 earnings call in July, Musk himself stated that people might have seen many viral humanoid robot demos online, but those performances were either scripted or remotely controlled. As a result, the market valuation for humanoid robot

companies has shifted from being viewed as tech firms to being seen as makers of large remote-controlled toys. This explains a phenomenon we’ve seen in many popular domestic videos featuring humanoid robots: there’s always a

staff member standing nearby with their hands behind their back, hidden under a cloth, seemingly busy with something. Another video revealed someone lifting that cloth, only to find an operator using a remote control underneath.

Viewers have commented that they should just operate the robots openly, instead of hiding and sneaking around. When you factor in the valuation shifts in capital markets, you'll understand why they are so ashamed to let you know

these humanoid robots are being remote-controlled. We call this "remote control shame," a cold trick that isn't an invention of our time. It actually dates back to 1770, when Wolfgang von Kempelen built "The Turk," a

chess-playing machine. It was a puppet in Turkish robes and a turban, sitting behind a cabinet and moving its arms. It beat Benjamin Franklin and reportedly defeated Napoleon. This machine was exhibited intermittently

across Europe and the US before being destroyed in a fire; only then was the truth fully revealed. The secret was that a real human chess player was hiding inside the cabinet. The current state of humanoid robots points to one

thing: the "brain" is truly difficult to develop, and they really can't figure it out yet. In another corner that no one pays attention to, robot brains are actually advancing very rapidly, and that is in the field of

special-purpose robots. Robots don't have to be humanoid; they can be wheeled, quadrupedal, or even snake-like. These forms are more pragmatic and designed for actual work, but they are rarely used for shows. The public rarely encounters information

about them, so their popularity is naturally quite low. Among these forms, quadruped robots currently have both volume and revenue. They are used in industrial inspection, manufacturing, disaster relief, and security, with

clients including power grids, public security bureaus, state-owned enterprises, and government departments . A Frost&Sullivan white paper in September indicated that Unitree, Deep Robotics, CloudMinds, and Cheetah Robotics make up the domestic front

line, known in the industry as the " four domestic puppies." Among them, Cheetah Robotics currently occupies a higher value position because it specializes in industrial inspection for the energy, chemical, and oil&gas

sectors, with the advantage of handling high-temperature, high-pressure, toxic, flammable, and explosive high-risk environments. However, the core behind these quadruped robots remains the " brain," which in this case is the world

model from Cheetah Robotics. It is this world model that allows these robots to actually work and complete tasks, which in turn leads to so many real orders. So, what exactly is this model? Before a quadruped robot takes a step, it

needs to know what will happen when it lands, whether the path is traversable, and if a rise in ambient temperature could cause an explosion. A world model is what handles this prediction. It builds a copy of the real world inside

the AI's brain, allowing the robot to simulate the scenario in the copy first before acting in the real world. In the name, "world" refers to this copy, and "model" means it can calculate the next move. Thus, a world model must do three

things: create a copy of the real scene , perform simulations within that copy, and apply the results to the real machine. Actually, the industry has been doing the first part for over a decade, known as digital twins. By

replicating the real factory in a computer and syncing real-time data, the computer becomes a mirror of the present; it only manages the replication step. A world model adds the next two steps—inference and transfer—to the mirror. It answers

the question, "If the robot moves this way, what will happen next?" It is a simulation of the future. But that’s still not enough. Taking Unitree’s world model as an example, it is the first embodied AI platform for

industrial robotics. Simply put, it integrates simulation, training, and real-machine data into a whole, adding a fourth step: feeding data from the field back to refine the copy. The process is easy to understand. It first

builds a one-to-one digital twin of the factory, using 3D technology to clone the site in a computer. Then, the quadruped robot practices in this replica, where the simulation module automatically generates a navigation

network and plans paths. It considers thousands of negative scenarios, such as slippery surfaces, nighttime fog, sensors covered in oil, or sudden ground collapse. Through learning, it selects the most stable route and

deploys the trained strategy to the real robot, allowing it to start working on-site. Finally, the images, temperatures, gas concentrations, and equipment sounds collected on-site are fed back into the virtual space via a

cloud platform, where they are compared and used to correct the twin before redeploying. The digital and real worlds constantly exchange data; the longer the system is used, the better it understands the factory, and the

more capable the robot becomes. In this process, the SevenSpring model is responsible for seeing, understanding, and judging—reading dials to determine if values are correct, detecting equipment failures through images and sounds, and inferring

potential issues from ambient noise— while the S 75.0 competence station handles motion control and navigation path planning, with algorithms iterating bidirectionally between simulation and real-world hardware. SevenSpring even addressed the

customer's operational experience by removing all the complex buttons from traditional monitoring screens, leaving only a dialogue box on the interface. It is precisely this successfully bridged path from technology to

practical application that has allowed these industrial world models to enter a positive feedback loop. Taking the SevenSpring model as an example: First, their data comes from the customers ' actual plant sites. While robots

perform dangerous tasks for humans, they also capture the work environment —providing the very kind of data the entire industry lacks most: real, continuous, and massive. SevenSpring secures orders, and each order continuously provides them with

invaluable data. This data makes their model stronger, and a stronger model attracts more customers, which in turn leads to more real-world data—and thus, the snowball effect begins. Second: scene matching. The factory

layout is fixed, and the routes are fixed, solving the generalization challenges mentioned earlier. While world models may still be a fantasy in household settings, they are already functional within fixed factory sites, allowing for "training before

deployment" and keeping danger out of the real world. You cannot create fires in reality, but you can experience millions of crises in the virtual world ; by the time the robot enters the site , it has already handled every

potential emergency and is an expert from day one. Fourth: build once, use repeatedly on one platform. The same virtual factory can run this robot just as well as that one. Whether it's a chemical plant today or a battery

factory tomorrow, there's no need to start from scratch when adjusting parameters or modifying lines. In the past, this industry relied on one-off solutions for every project, making it impossible to scale. Now, with usable

3D assets and replicable deployment processes, it is finally possible to scale up. Fifth: massive demand. There are 23,000 major hazardous chemical sites, 200,000 kilometers of oil and gas pipelines, and 2.64 million kilometers of high-voltage lines

nationwide, all of which are legally required to be inspected. Previously, this relied on manual labor, which was inefficient. Now, major state-owned enterprises and the government are accelerating action; for instance, the

State Grid and the "three oil giants" have begun purchasing, and policy is also driving it, as seen in the national directives issued in May. The "AI plus Energy" two-way empowerment action plan specifically calls for

tackling dedicated large-model agents for energy. On May 26, embodied intelligence released 51 high-value scenarios. The Ministry of Industry and Information Technology's AI-driven manufacturing document this year

highlights world models, and Shengtong happens to be in this window. Looking back, we realize that the current robot brain competition's true main battlefield isn't the exhibition matches of humanoid robots, but rather

industrial robots competing with world models. This answers the title: today’s humanoid robots, as a performance business, truly only deserve to be called toys. Even the concept of a "humanoid robot" can be understood from another angle. Let me

ask you, your robot vacuum at home doesn't need to look human, right? Your laundry robot at home doesn't need to look human either, right? Furthermore, your home computing robot doesn't seem to need to look human, does it? And

your information delivery robot, your food storage robot—none of them seem to need to look human, right? Tools and robots are clearly not the same thing. Imagine if I made a washing machine humanoid and called it a laundry robot,

or turned a fridge into a humanoid and called it a food storage robot—would you accept them as true robots? So, what are we so enthusiastically discussing when we talk about the robot industry? When we set our standards

high enough, we finally touch the core of this industry. At first, everyone said making them humanoid was because our human society was built for the human body; stairs, door handles, and valves are all designed for creatures

with two legs and two arms, so humanoid robots are more versatile. But my assessment is that this is just sophisticated sophistry, highly misleading. It sounds logical, but being humanoid is inherently a empty promise. It implicitly suggests that by

making a robot humanoid, it is meant to take on all the work humans do. But this promise fails to tell you if it actually has an intelligent enough brain. Yet, humans are more accepting of humanoids, find it easier to form

emotional connections, and find it easier to generate buzz; the goal of creating buzz is certainly achieved. Meanwhile, looking at the less flashy side, the robot industry's brain upgrades have a more pragmatic direction than just pleasing audiences

on stage: doing dangerous tasks for humans. It’s just that this direction isn't flashy and can't make it onto a primetime variety show. Humanoid robots always get applause and attention; conversely, for robots that brave

danger and perform disaster relief, the best news is, instead...
